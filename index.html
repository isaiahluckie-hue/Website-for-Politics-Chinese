3Problems this site weighs: competition, cost, and security

Today the United States has effectively shut Chinese electric vehicles out of its market through two overlapping policies. The first is a 100 percent tariff, raised from 25 percent in May 2024. The second is the Commerce Department’s Connected Vehicle Rule, which bars vehicles that use Chinese or Russian connectivity hardware or software, and bars Chinese- or Russian-controlled automakers from selling connected vehicles here unless the government authorizes them.

On the surface, exclusion looks like a response to a single threat: Chinese competition. In practice, the tariff and the rule answer different problems, and they are defended as a single package without weighing the costs and benefits of each. This site weighs them against three problems, shown below.

The question is live in Washington. A group representing nearly all major automakers has urged Congress to pass legislation permanently barring Chinese vehicles, yet in September 2026 President Trump said he would not oppose Chinese automakers building cars in the United States if they hire American workers.

One case shows how far the policy reaches

Polestar assembles its Polestar 3 SUV in South Carolina, yet beginning with model year 2027 it will not be allowed to sell new cars in the United States.

The Commerce Department declined to authorize it under the Connected Vehicle Rule. Polestar’s parent, Geely, also controls Volvo, which was granted a special authorization to keep selling connected vehicles here. The case raises a question Washington has not settled: whether exclusion is the best way to serve American industry, consumers, and security.

Audience

This site is addressed to Congress and the executive branch. Congress can write the broad terms. The U.S. Trade Representative administers the Section 301 tariff, and Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security administers the Connected Vehicle Rule and decides which companies receive authorization to sell.

Evidence

What the research shows

Competition: unfair or earned?

Chinese automakers have become formidable competitors quickly. China accounted for about 60 percent of global EV sales by 2025, and a modeling study estimates that if Chinese EVs entered the U.S. market at a low tariff, they would take nearly 70 percent of the U.S. EV market and displace about 87,000 manufacturing jobs. Cheaper cars benefit buyers, so the question is how to weigh producers’ losses against consumers’ gains.

Price of BYD Seal vs. Tesla Model 3, late 2022 to late 2025

Scott Kennedy estimates Chinese government support for EVs at $230.9 billion from 2009 to 2023 and argues it has tilted the playing field. Rhodium Group finds that subsidies explain only about 5 percent of BYD’s roughly $4,700 per-vehicle cost advantage over Tesla, while vertical integration and lower overhead account for at least three-quarters. BYD makes about 80 percent of its major components in-house, versus 37 percent for Tesla, and Rhodium finds no clear evidence that Chinese firms are sacrificing margins to undercut rivals.

That weakens the claim that Chinese pricing is mainly unfair, since anti-subsidy tools address government support, not structural cost advantages. But subsidies are a growing part of the picture: BYD’s subsidy income rose from 26 percent of its net income in 2024 to 35 percent in 2025. And an earned advantage is still a threat to U.S. producers, because the gap would be even larger against Western automakers producing outside China.

Cost: who pays?

Exclusion’s cost is mostly options Americans never get to choose. Only about 9 percent of new U.S. vehicle sales are EVs, compared with 54 percent in China, and EVs from incumbent automakers cost about $9,500 more than comparable gasoline cars.

EV share of new vehicle sales

The closest precedent is the 1981 Japanese export restraint. Robert Feenstra found that about two-thirds of the rise in import prices reflected quality upgrading, leaving a price increase of about 3 percent that consumers were not compensated for, and the restraint added only about 5,600 to 11,100 U.S. auto jobs in its first year, no more than about 5 percent of the layoffs the industry had already suffered.

The strongest modern evidence is a 2026 modeling study that scores each policy by adding up what buyers gain, what automakers earn, the value of lower emissions, and tariff revenue.

Barwick and coauthors (2026), a working paper based on modeling. Its tally does not count security risks.

Allowing Chinese EVs in produces net gains under every scenario, and every scenario loses manufacturing jobs. Completely excluding Chinese EVs sacrifices significant economic and consumer benefits, while a more balanced policy could preserve some domestic manufacturing support without eliminating competition.

Security: what a tariff cannot solve

A tariff taxes imports, but it does not govern software or data. The Connected Vehicle Rule does. Commerce is concerned that connected vehicles from China could allow mass collection of sensitive information, including location, audio, and video recordings, and a Chinese law requires Chinese-registered companies to provide data to the government on request, wherever they operate.

Same parent, different outcome

Geely controls Volvo (authorized) and Polestar (denied). The Polestar 3 is built in South Carolina.

The evidence shows capability, not misuse. A Swedish national China centre overview finds no direct public evidence of the Chinese government extracting data from connected vehicles, and a Leiden report finds Chinese smart vehicles generally meet international standards while China’s data law remains ambiguous. CSIS argues the risk is undeniable. Data collection is also an industry-wide problem: a privacy foundation found that every one of 25 car brands it reviewed collects more personal data than necessary.
